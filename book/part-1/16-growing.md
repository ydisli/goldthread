The previous fifteen chapters described techniques. This one is about the person using them: how to organise your week so the important work happens, the habits that make you better over time, and how to keep growing once you're competent.

## A weekly rhythm

POs and BAs are pulled in every direction: stakeholder messages, team questions, meetings, incidents, requests. Without a rhythm, the urgent crowds out the important, and the important things in this job (discovery, thinking, writing clearly, measuring) are rarely urgent.

A rhythm that works for many people:

**Every day (30 minutes, first thing):**
- Check what needs you: approvals, requests to triage, questions from the team, your action items, mentions.
- Glance at what's coming up this week and any promises at risk.
- Answer the team's questions before your meetings start.

**Weekly:**
- **Stakeholder touchpoints** by cadence (Chapter 4). Prep each one for five minutes.
- **Refinement** with the team (Chapter 11).
- **Forecast and RAID review**: has anything changed? Any promises to renegotiate? (Chapter 12)
- **Discovery time**: at least two hours with real evidence: tickets, an interview, an observation, data.
- **Friday review**: what moved, what didn't, what you learned, what you'll focus on next week. Fifteen minutes, written.

**Monthly:**
- Stakeholder map review: has anyone's power, interest or engagement changed?
- Backlog hygiene pass.
- Metric tree check: are the inputs moving?

**Quarterly:**
- OKR review and planning.
- Bet outcomes due: measure, record, learn (Chapter 15).
- Calibration check: how did your confidence compare with results?

> [!tip] Protect thinking time
> Block two mornings a week with no meetings. Use them for writing, analysis and discovery. If your calendar is full of other people's meetings, you're doing coordination, not product work. Coordination matters, but it isn't the job.

## Habits that compound

Small habits, practised consistently, separate good POs and BAs from great ones over a few years:

- **Write decisions down the same day.** Two sentences in the decision log. Future you will be grateful.
- **Ask "how will we know?"** for every piece of work. If there's no answer, the work isn't ready.
- **Close loops.** Every request, every promise, every question you said you'd answer. Track them and close them.
- **Bring bad news early, with options.** Every time.
- **Read the tickets.** Ten minutes a week reading raw service desk tickets about your product will teach you more than most meetings.
- **Show your work.** Make your reasoning visible: the priorities, the forecast, the decision records. Visible reasoning invites challenge, and challenge makes it better.
- **Say "I don't know yet, I'll find out by Thursday"** instead of guessing.

## Influence without authority

POs and BAs rarely have formal authority over the people they need. The engineers don't report to you. The stakeholders certainly don't. Your influence comes from three sources:

- **Expertise:** you know the problem, the users and the evidence better than anyone. This is why discovery matters for your influence, not just for the product.
- **Trust:** you've been reliable, honest and fair over time (Chapter 4).
- **Clarity:** you make complicated things simple. A clear one-page problem statement or a well-drawn process model moves decisions more than any amount of persuasion.

When you need someone to do something, help them see how it serves **their** interests. Dana supports JML once it makes the audit easier, not because Alex asked nicely.

## Working with engineers

The relationship with the engineering team is the one that most shapes your day. Some principles:

- **Bring problems, not solutions.** Engineers are expert problem-solvers. Give them the problem, the constraints and the evidence, and let them propose solutions. You'll often get better ones than you'd have specified.
- **Respect technical work.** Technical debt, upgrades and reliability work aren't distractions from the roadmap; they're what keeps the roadmap possible. Make room for them and help explain their value to stakeholders (Chapter 6).
- **Be in the room for estimation,** not to negotiate numbers down but to answer questions that change the size.
- **Share the context.** Tell the team why priorities are what they are, what stakeholders said, what the metrics show. Teams that understand the why make better small decisions every day.
- **Celebrate outcomes, not just releases.** When the password ticket numbers fall, show the team the graph.

## Working with AI assistants

AI assistants are now part of most knowledge work, including product and analysis work. Used well, they make you faster and more thorough. Used badly, they produce confident, generic, wrong documents at scale.

Where they help:

- **As a reviewer.** Ask an assistant to review your spec as a tester, a security reviewer or a service desk agent would. It's good at finding missing exceptions, vague words and untested paths. You decide which questions matter.
- **As a summariser.** Turning meeting notes into decisions, actions and risks; condensing a long ticket export into themes. Check the output against the source.
- **As a sparring partner.** "What are the strongest arguments against this prioritisation?" "What exceptions might a joiner–mover–leaver process have?" It widens your thinking.
- **As a drafter of first versions** of routine text (release notes, update emails), which you then edit with your knowledge of the audience.
- **As an interface to your data.** If your workspace is connected (Throughline supports this through MCP), an assistant can search your stakeholders, specs and decisions, prepare you for a meeting, or capture a note, using your real context rather than generic knowledge.

Where to be careful:

- **It doesn't know your organisation.** Requirements, priorities and stakeholder judgements depend on context the model doesn't have unless you give it. Generic output is usually wrong in the details that matter.
- **It can be confidently wrong.** Check facts, numbers and especially anything about regulations.
- **Privacy.** Don't paste personal data, confidential information or credentials into tools your organisation hasn't approved. Prefer tools that redact personal data before sending anything to a model, and check what your organisation allows.
- **It can't take accountability.** A decision or requirement is yours, whoever drafted it. "The AI suggested it" isn't a rationale.

The best mental model: an assistant is a fast, well-read colleague who has never met your stakeholders. Use it to think more broadly and work faster; keep the judgement.

## Career paths

There's no single path, but common directions include:

- **Product Owner →** senior PO with a larger product or platform → **product manager** or **group product manager** owning strategy across teams → head of product.
- **Business Analyst →** senior or lead BA → **enterprise or business architect**, **product owner/manager**, **process or transformation lead**, or BA practice lead.
- **Sideways moves** between the two are common and valuable: a BA who becomes a PO brings rigour; a PO who learns analysis brings clarity.

Certifications (from the Scrum and business analysis bodies, among others) can structure your learning and help in some job markets. They're no substitute for practice. A portfolio of real problem statements, specs, decision records and measured outcomes says more about you than any certificate.

## A self-assessment

Use the five responsibilities from Chapter 1 to assess yourself honestly. For each, where are you today?

| Responsibility | Developing | Capable | Strong |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Know the problem and the people** | Relies on requesters' descriptions | Writes evidenced problem statements; maps stakeholders | Finds problems before they're raised; stakeholders seek them out |
| **Make the work clear** | Writes stories that need lots of clarification | Testable requirements, models where needed, few requirement defects | Others reuse their specs and models as examples |
| **Make choices** | Orders by who asked loudest | Uses cost of delay and evidence; explains priorities | Says no often, and people accept it because the reasoning is visible |
| **Keep promises honest** | Gives single dates, surprises people | Forecasts with ranges; renegotiates early | Stakeholders trust their dates; no surprises |
| **Check that it worked** | Moves on after release | Measures outcomes; records results | Calibrated confidence; learning visibly changes how they bet |

Pick **one** cell to move up this quarter. Choose the technique from this book that addresses it, practise it deliberately, and look for evidence that it changed something.

## Looking after yourself

These roles sit at the junction of many people's needs, and it's easy to absorb all the pressure. The hero who keeps everything in their head, answers every message at night and never says no is not doing the job well. They're creating a single point of failure and heading for burnout.

Write things down so they don't live only in your head. Share context so others can answer questions. Say no to work that doesn't serve the strategy, including your own pet ideas. And take your holidays: a team that can't function for two weeks without you has a process problem, not a dedication problem.

- Have it write the full spec from a one-line description and send it for sign-off
- * Ask it to review your spec as a tester and a security reviewer would, then decide which of its questions matter
- Paste in the HR export with employee names so it has full context
- Let it decide the priority of the requirements
Why: Assistants are good reviewers and sparring partners. They lack your organisational context and can't take accountability, and personal data shouldn't be pasted into unapproved tools.
